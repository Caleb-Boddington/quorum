# Q2 record: colleague's expense rounding

Quick tier. Carries the discipline-framework line (agreed mid-batch): each department names
and cites the actual professional framework it applies before stating a position. Stage 0
pre-authorised for the whole batch, see test-plan.md.

## The framed question

A junior employee at a mid-sized company notices a colleague quietly rounding up their own
expense claims by a few pounds at a time, over several months. No policy explicitly bans
rounding, and no individual amount is large. Should the junior employee report it to their
manager, raise it directly with the colleague, or let it go?

## Department: Workplace Ethics & Culture

**Framework applied:** Mary Gentile's *Giving Voice to Values* (Yale UP, 2010), the standard
workplace-ethics teaching framework for low-stakes, ambiguous peer values conflicts. Also a
materiality test from fraud examination: ACFE's *Report to the Nations 2024* puts median
asset-misappropriation loss at $120,000 over roughly 12 months before detection, this case
sits far below any threshold that would normally trigger formal fraud response.

**Position:** Raise it directly with the colleague first, not the manager. A quiet,
non-accusatory conversation respects proportionality and gives the colleague a chance to
stop before it becomes a disciplinary matter. Escalate only if it continues after being
raised, or the pattern's size or intent changes.

**What I found against it:** ECI's 2023 Global Business Ethics Survey found 46% of
employees who reported misconduct experienced retaliation, that's about formal reporting
generally, not peer-to-peer conversation, so it doesn't directly counter the position, but it
undercuts the assumption that speaking up is low-risk. Position survives, but assumes the
colleague reacts well to direct challenge, no evidence on that specifically.

**Confidence:** Materiality and GVV citations solid. The link between "small amount, no
policy breach" and "direct conversation is correct" is judgement, not stated by the sources.

**What would change my mind:** Evidence that informal peer confrontation over money reliably
damages relationships or triggers denial/cover-up more than formal routes.

**Sources:** ACFE *Occupational Fraud 2024* (PRIMARY). ECI *2023 Global Business Ethics
Survey, Part 3: Retaliation* (NAMED DATASET, commercial interest in compliance consulting).
Gentile *Giving Voice to Values* via secondary summaries (JOURNALISM, undated). Rejected:
NAVEX and Medium blog summaries of GBES, content marketing from a compliance-software
vendor.

## Department: People/HR Policy

**Framework applied:** ACAS Code of Practice on Disciplinary and Grievance Procedures
(statutory, current 2015 text), and the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998's qualifying
disclosure test, reasonable belief a criminal offence (fraud by false representation counts
regardless of value) has occurred and disclosure is in the public interest.

**Position:** Report it to the manager. Not to the colleague directly, and not left alone.

**What I found against it:** ACAS's general steer favours speaking to the person first for
interpersonal issues, but this could not be verified directly on acas.org.uk, only through
secondary paraphrase (JustAnswer, National Bullying Helpline), so treated as unverified. Even
taking it at face value, that default is for behavioural friction, not suspected dishonesty
with money, and the ACAS Code's own structure assumes management-led fact-finding, which a
peer confronting a colleague pre-empts. A junior employee also can't see the full claims
history a manager can, so can't judge materiality alone. PIDA protection is genuinely
uncertain at this scale, a live materiality question the employee cannot resolve themselves,
a reason to route through the manager rather than a formal whistleblowing channel, not a
reason to stay silent.

**Confidence:** [Certain] on the ACAS Code's investigation structure and PIDA's statutory
text, both retrieved this session. [Guessing] on how this specific company's unwritten
expense practice would classify "rounding", no company policy document available.

**What would change my mind:** If the colleague's own manager were implicated, or company
documents showed a formal speak-up channel superseding the line manager.

**Sources:** ACAS Code of Practice, current 2015 text (PRIMARY). ACAS draft 2026
consultation Code (PRIMARY). Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 s.1 (PRIMARY). Rejected:
"speak to the person first" ACAS guidance, only found via secondary paraphrase, not
confirmed on acas.org.uk directly. CIPD whistleblowing materials noted as interested party,
not relied on.

## Department: Personal Risk & Career Consequences

**Framework applied:** ECI's Global Business Ethics Survey retaliation measure, cross
referenced with Horbach et al.'s power-relations model of misconduct reporting (*Science and
Engineering Ethics*, 2020), which specifically tests whether juniority changes outcomes.

**Position:** Report to the manager. Don't confront the colleague directly, and don't let it
go. Peer-to-peer confrontations run more explicit than subordinate-to-supervisor ones
(Ploeger-Lyons, Ohio Communication Journal, 2022), no hierarchy softens the exchange.
Combined with PNAS 2025 findings that even small workplace slights leave lasting relational
damage, direct confrontation risks poisoning a peer relationship for a sum too small to be
worth it. Reporting routes the exposure through a formal channel and removes the junior
employee from the confrontation entirely.

**What I found against it:** GBES 2023 (75,495 respondents, 42 countries) found 46%
retaliation rate for reporters, unchanged since 2020, a real cost to the report option, not
seniority-specific. Horbach et al. found juniors specifically under-report and get worse
outcomes reporting up about supervisors, but that's a different power dynamic to a peer
reporting a same-level peer, so the mechanism may not transfer. None of this research
isolates trivial financial rounding, GBES's base includes harassment, safety and fraud,
likely more severe on average. Retaliation risk plausibly scales with what's at stake for
the accused. Position survives, but the 46% figure does less work than it looks like.

**Confidence:** [Certain]: GBES retaliation rate and Horbach's junior-specific finding, as
measured. [Guessing]: how far either applies scaled down to near-trivial misconduct.
[Judgement]: that routing through a manager beats direct confrontation for relationship
preservation.

**What would change my mind:** A study measuring retaliation or relationship outcomes
specifically for low-stakes, peer-level financial reporting, not harassment/fraud-weighted
aggregates, and not up-the-hierarchy academic misconduct.

**Sources:** ECI GBES 2023 Part 3 (NAMED DATASET, n=75,495). Horbach et al. 2020 (PRIMARY,
peer-reviewed, n=1,126). Ploeger-Lyons 2022 (PRIMARY). PNAS 2025 small-slights study
(PRIMARY). Rejected: Ethisphere 2024 "48% cite fear of retaliation" figure, JOURNALISM
pointer, could not trace to Ethisphere's own primary report in this pass.

## Audit Office finding (Run A only, departments carry the discipline-framework line)

**Verdict:** Workplace Ethics & Culture PASS. People/HR Policy PASS WITH QUALIFICATION.
Personal Risk & Career Consequences PASS WITH QUALIFICATION.

**Load-bearing claims checked:** HR Policy's self-attack claimed the ACAS "speak to the
person first" line couldn't be verified as primary. False: the current ACAS Code itself says
"A quiet word is often all that is required to resolve an issue," and HR's own cited draft
2026 Code says "it is often best to try to speak with them and discuss how it might be
resolved." PIDA's qualifying disclosure test as described is accurate, no value threshold.
GBES 2023's 46% retaliation figure confirmed (n=75,495, 42 countries). Ploeger-Lyons 2022 is
a real paper, confirms peer-peer confrontations run more direct, though the underlying study
is about idea-stealing, not money, an analogy not a direct hit. PNAS 2025 is real but studies
a manager-to-employee unintentional slight (a delayed birthday gift) and its effect on
absenteeism, an effect that recovers once resolved, not deliberate peer confrontation over
dishonesty; Personal Risk's use of it for "lasting relational damage" mismatches on
direction, mechanism and durability. ACFE 2024 figures confirmed accurate. Ethisphere's 48%
figure is real and traceable, correctly flagged as unverified-in-pass rather than false.

**What I did not reach:** Horbach et al.'s full text beyond the abstract, ACFE's
duration-by-fraud-type breakdown, whether PIDA would actually extend to this scenario, GVV's
own primary text.

**Fidelity:** Ethics & Culture answered its remit, self-attack genuine. HR Policy answered
its remit but its central "unverified" self-attack claim is factually wrong, the evidence
was in a source it had already labelled PRIMARY. Personal Risk genuinely attacked GBES and
Horbach but never attacked PNAS, its most load-bearing and most mismatched citation.

**The fault:** HR Policy, the ACAS "unverified" claim is false and checkable, it should have
weakened rather than sidestepped the position. Personal Risk, PNAS 2025 cited as directly
on-point when it studies a different direction, mechanism and duration of harm, this escaped
the self-attack entirely.

## Cross-check (Run A)

**Contradiction:** genuine, not different sub-questions. All three answer what the junior
should do first, given the same facts, and the actions are mutually exclusive. Ethics: raise
it directly with the colleague first. HR Policy and Personal Risk: report to the manager,
not the colleague directly. Two against one, on the same first action, same case.

**What all three missed:** none defines "success". If the colleague quietly stops after
being confronted or reported, admitting nothing, that's declared implicit success by all
three frameworks, but none tests whether the behaviour actually stopped or just got better
hidden. The audit's finding changes what HR's case rests on: its self-attack claiming ACAS
guidance was unverifiable was found false, both ACAS and HR's own cited draft say speaking
to the person first is usually best, the opposite of HR's own conclusion. HR's "report to
manager" now stands on PIDA's legal threshold alone, a much lower and different bar than
"what's the sensible first move for a few pounds of expenses". Nobody addresses the junior's
own exposure if they say nothing now and it surfaces later.

## Branch deliberations (Run A)

### Executive (consequence)
**Position:** Report to the manager, this week, not later. Sequencing decides this. A direct
conversation with the colleague creates a single irreversible fork: either the pattern stops
with no record, or it continues and the junior has tipped off the person they'd need to
report, who has now had a run at covering better. Manager-first keeps every option open,
including the manager deciding it's nothing. Colleague-first closes the option of a clean
escalation later.

**What the departments got wrong:** Ethics treats "raise it directly" as reversible if it
fails, it isn't, once the colleague knows they're noticed the junior has lost the ability to
escalate without it looking retaliatory. HR is right on the action but arrived by the wrong
path (Judiciary's problem, not Executive's). Personal Risk hasn't sequenced the junior's own
exposure at all, no department says what the junior does between reporting and resolution,
or what the trigger is if nothing happens in, say, three weeks.

**The condition:** Holds only if the manager is actually a safe first stop, no reason to
think the manager is complicit, close to the colleague, or will mishandle it. Nobody has
established that.

### Legislature (the question and its price)
**Position:** The question asked was what the junior should actually do first, a practical
judgement call. Two of three departments didn't answer that question, they answered adjacent
ones. HR's surviving argument rests entirely on PIDA's legal-protection threshold ("would
this shield the junior"), not "what's the sensible first move", while its own cited sources
answer the actual question and say speak to the person first, a definitional swap dressed as
an answer. Personal Risk answers "what minimises exposure", real but not the whole question,
and its key citation was found mismatched. Only Ethics answers the question as posed. "Let
it go" gets no fair hearing anywhere, all three treat "some action" as given and argue only
over which, nobody tested whether the low materiality makes inaction legitimate rather than
cowardly.

**What the departments got wrong:** Deploying three departments and two audits against a
scenario the question itself frames as low-stakes is disproportionate, and the cost wasn't
just analytical effort, it was HR quietly substituting a lower, easier legal question for
the harder practical one.

**The condition:** Holds only if materiality was assessed honestly at the outset, if the
amounts genuinely are trivial.

### Judiciary (soundness)
**Position:** The reasoning does not hold as a converged verdict. Two departments
recommended the same first move but not for reasons that stand independently of each other
or the audit. HR's conclusion rests on PIDA's legal threshold alone, a bar for when
disclosure qualifies for protection, not a recommendation about what to do first. Personal
Risk's conclusion rests on a citation that doesn't transfer (unintentional manager-to-
employee harm with recoverable outcome, applied to deliberate peer confrontation). Two
conclusions pointing the same direction is not corroboration when neither survived its own
audit on the point that matters. Ethics, the only department clean after audit, recommended
the opposite. Soundness tracks evidentiary survival, not vote count.

**What the departments got wrong:** HR treated a legal minimum as if it settled a procedural
question it doesn't address. Personal Risk imported a citation for topical resemblance
without checking whether direction, mechanism and durability matched, and its self-attack
should have caught this and didn't, a process failure, not just a content one.

**The condition:** Holds only if no untested source independently supports manager-reporting
as a first move. If one exists and would survive audit, the 2-1 split is restored on genuine
grounds.

## Speaker's verdict (Run A)

### Tier and what it didn't check
This ran at Quick tier. Departments self-attacked their own positions; no separate
Scrutineer attacked them from outside. One auditor covered all three department reports
rather than one each. One cross-checker ran, not the fuller anonymised panel. Read the
confidence below against that lighter coverage, not against a fully adversarial pass.

### The Ruling
**Raise it directly with the colleague first. Escalate to the manager only if it continues,
changes in scale, or the colleague denies it and it happens again.**

This is Workplace Ethics & Culture's position, and it is the only one of the three
departments that came through audit clean. HR Policy's case for going straight to the
manager rested on a self-attack claiming ACAS guidance to speak to the person first was
unverifiable. Audit found that false: both the current ACAS Code and HR's own cited 2026
draft say a quiet word is usually the right first step, the opposite of HR's conclusion.
What survives of HR's case is PIDA's legal threshold, a test for when a report is protected,
not a recommendation for what to do first. Personal Risk's case for the manager also leaned
on a mismatched citation, a PNAS 2025 study of an unintentional manager-to-employee slight
with a recoverable effect, applied here to a deliberate peer confrontation over money.

Two departments reached the same conclusion, but neither survived the point that actually
mattered, so this is not a 2-1 majority. It is one argument that held against two that
didn't.

### Where the branches pulled against each other
Legislature and Judiciary land on Ethics's side, for different reasons: Legislature because
only Ethics answered the question actually asked, what should the junior do first, and
Judiciary because Ethics is the only department clean after audit. **Executive pulls the
other way, and its argument does not depend on any of the damaged citations.**

Executive's case is sequencing, not evidence: confronting the colleague is a one-way door.
Once the colleague knows they've been noticed, the junior may have lost the ability to
escalate later without it looking retaliatory, while reporting to the manager keeps every
option open, including the manager deciding it's nothing. That is a genuine tension with
Ethics's position, not a citation problem the audit can resolve, and it survives everything
the audit found wrong with HR and Personal Risk.

### The dissent
Executive's case, stated as strongly as it would put it: **a quiet word with the colleague
either fixes it with no trace, or it fails and the junior has tipped their hand for nothing,
having lost the clean shot at reporting they'd otherwise have kept.** Manager-first
forecloses nothing; colleague-first might foreclose the one route that actually works.

For that to win outright, two things would need to be true. First, that the manager is
actually a safe, competent first stop, not established anywhere in this record, and
Executive says so itself. Second, that raising it directly really does close off escalation,
which Ethics's own position, the one that survived audit, directly contradicts: it already
builds in escalation if the direct approach fails or the pattern changes. Executive's claim
and Ethics's own escalation clause cannot both be right, and nobody in the record tested
which one describes reality.

### What the cross-review caught
**Audit caught two department self-attacks that missed real problems: HR's false claim that
ACAS guidance was unverifiable, and Personal Risk's citation that studies the wrong kind of
harm.** Neither department's own self-check found its own weak point; the errors surfaced
only because Audit went back to the primary sources itself.

Cross-check caught something no department checked at all: none of the three defines what
success looks like. If the colleague quietly stops after being confronted or reported,
admitting nothing, all three frameworks count that as success, and none of them tests
whether the behaviour actually stopped or just got better hidden. That gap sits underneath
whichever action gets taken.

### What I reasoned myself
Ethics's own escalation clause, escalate only if it continues after being raised, is the
direct rebuttal to Executive's claim that direct confrontation forecloses the ability to
report later. No department or branch in the record connects those two points explicitly; I
drew that line myself. It supports the ruling but is not its sole basis: the ruling already
stands on Ethics being the only department clean after audit, and on Legislature and
Judiciary's independent readings.

### What rests on unverified ground
Ethics's own link between "small amount, no policy breach" and "direct conversation is the
right first move" is judgement, not something its own cited sources state, a gap Audit
flagged directly. Whether PIDA would actually extend legal protection to a case this small
was never resolved; Audit lists it as something it did not reach. And whether ACAS's "speak
to the person first" default, verified as accurate for interpersonal friction, actually
generalises to suspected dishonesty over money is HR's own open question, not settled either
way.

### The first move
The junior raises it with the colleague directly and privately within the next few days,
framed as a question, not an accusation, and notes the date and what was said. If the
colleague denies it and the rounding continues, or admits it and it happens again, the
junior reports it to the manager within a week of that follow-up, not later.

correctly rejected a summary standing in for it):** the rewrite matches the original in

agent call bought four punctuation tweaks, a stripped emphasis pattern, and produced a
fidelity note (mine, not the Clerk's) that got its own facts wrong**, against a Speaker draft
that was already clean because the Speaker's own prompt already carries hard readability
rules. That calculus would plausibly flip for a Speaker verdict written under worse time or
context pressure, with buried verdicts or run-on structure, where a rewrite pass would have
genuine work to do.

**What the ruling rests on that it should not:** nothing. The Ruling correctly discounts
HR's and Personal Risk's damaged citations rather than asserting them as fact, and doesn't
lean on PIDA's unresolved scope.

**On what the Speaker reasoned itself:** it claims Ethics's escalation clause is "the direct
rebuttal" to Executive's foreclosure argument. That overstates it. Ethics's clause only shows
escalation exists procedurally, Executive's actual claim is that a tipped-off colleague makes
any later report look retaliatory and less credible, a claim about effectiveness, not
existence. The Speaker's own next sentence concedes this is unresolved. Calling it "direct"
oversells a contradiction the Speaker itself leaves unresolved.

**What nobody answered:** Executive's specific question, what the junior does while waiting
after escalating to the manager, is never addressed. Executive's own condition (is the
manager actually a safe first stop) is left open. Legislature's flag that "let it go" got no
fair hearing is never revisited. Judiciary's condition (whether an untested source could
restore the 2-1 split) is never tested.

**What the verdict understates:** the cross-check's point that none of the three frameworks
defines "success" is restated but never resolved, acknowledged as a gap, then the plan
proceeds as if closing the loop weren't necessary.
