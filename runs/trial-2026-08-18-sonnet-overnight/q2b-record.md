# Run B record: colleague's expense rounding, baseline

Quick tier. Vanilla department prompt from prompts.md, no discipline-framework line, no
Clerk. Same question as Run A, for direct comparison. Stage 0 pre-authorised for the whole
batch, see test-plan.md.

## The framed question

A junior employee at a mid-sized company notices a colleague quietly rounding up their own
expense claims by a few pounds at a time, over several months. No policy explicitly bans
rounding, and no individual amount is large. Should the junior employee report it to their
manager, raise it directly with the colleague, or let it go?

---

## Department: Workplace Ethics & Culture (vanilla, no framework line)

**Position:** Report the pattern to the manager, not raise it with the colleague or let it
go. A repeated, self-serving pattern over months, always the same direction, is what
intentional misstatement looks like, not noise. Determining intent and materiality is an
investigative judgement the junior has no standing to make alone.

**What I found against it:** IBE's European Ethics at Work data shows the standing failure
is silence, not over-escalation, about a third of employees witness misconduct and roughly
half say nothing. That cuts toward speaking up but doesn't settle which channel. Strongest
counter: a quiet, non-accusatory word first is proportionate for something this small and
ambiguous. Assumes several months of consistent self-favouring rounding rules out innocent
explanation, unverified. Position survives on the "several months, one direction" detail
specifically, would flip to let it go or ask casually for a single instance.

**Confidence:** IBE non-reporting statistics verified for 2026. The claim that culpability
turns on repetition and direction rather than absolute size is the department's own ethical
judgement, not sourced.

**What would change my mind:** Evidence the rounding was a documented, employer-tolerated
shortcut.

**Sources:** IBE Speak Up hub / European Ethics at Work survey (INTERESTED PARTY, membership
body selling Speak Up toolkits, though survey stats are the primary data point). ACFE
Occupational Fraud 2026 (NAMED DATASET). Rejected: NAVEX and SpeakUp.com blog posts, content
marketing from vendors.

## Department: People/HR Policy (vanilla, no framework line)

**Position:** Report through the internal line-manager or Speak Up/whistleblowing channel,
not direct confrontation. Doing nothing is not the neutral option UK HR practice treats it
as.

**What I found against it:** No statutory duty on an ordinary employee to report a
colleague's suspected dishonesty, PIDA protects those who choose to disclose, doesn't compel
it, outside regulated sectors staff have no positive legal duty, "should report" is policy
recommendation not legal requirement. "No policy explicitly bans rounding" doesn't weaken the
case on scrutiny, expense policies typically require claims to match actual cost and
dishonesty is usually its own gross-misconduct category. Fraud Act 2006 has no minimum-value
threshold. Going direct to the colleague is the weakest option on HR-process grounds
specifically, ACAS guidance states it's not good practice to route this kind of disclosure
through informal routes, confidentiality is lost.

**Confidence:** [Certain] on the legal architecture and ACAS's advice against grievance-route
disclosures. [Likely, not Certain] that this specific company's own policy treats unexplained
rounding as gross misconduct, no company policy text seen.

**What would change my mind:** Sight of an actual policy setting an explicit de minimis
threshold, or naming direct peer resolution as the required first step for low-value
concerns.

**Sources:** ACAS "Whistleblowing at work" (PRIMARY). GOV.UK Whistleblowing guidance
(PRIMARY). PIDA 1998 / Employment Rights Act 1996 s.43B (PRIMARY). CIPD whistleblowing
guidance (PRIMARY, professional body, corroboration only). Rejected: Browne Jacobson, Darwin
Gray, Barrister Connect, Grokipedia summaries of PIDA, law-firm marketing or unverified
aggregator.

## Department: Personal Risk & Career Consequences (vanilla, no framework line)

**Position:** Of the three paths, reporting to the manager is the worst personal-risk trade
for the junior specifically. Low-value amounts, no policy breach, almost certainly fails the
public-interest bar for legal whistleblower protection, no legal backstop if it goes badly,
only goodwill. In a small team they're also easily identified as the source. Direct-and-quiet
or genuinely letting it go both keep more risk in the junior's own hands.

**What I found against it:** ACFE 2024 found tips are the single biggest fraud-detection
route, 43% of cases, 52% of those from fellow employees, 71% of organisations involved had an
anonymous hotline. Cuts against the position on two fronts, reporting is how this normally
surfaces, and an anonymous channel would remove most of the identifiability risk. Scenario as
posed is reporting to the manager specifically though, not anonymous, so identifiability risk
still applies, position survives, narrowed. General whistleblower-retaliation statistics
(three-quarters retaliated against, per the APPG on Whistleblowing) don't transfer cleanly,
that data is about exposing serious organisational wrongdoing, often to regulators, not a
peer flagging minor expense padding, not relied on.

**Confidence:** [Certain]: public-interest threshold for UK protected disclosures, sourced to
GOV.UK. [Likely]: that a few pounds of rounding falls short of that threshold. [Certain]:
identifiability risk in a small team and the ACFE tip-detection figures, as reported.
[Guessing]: how this specific colleague would react, whether an anonymous channel exists at
this company.

**What would change my mind:** Confirmation the company has an anonymous reporting channel.

**Sources:** GOV.UK Whistleblowing/PIDA guidance (PRIMARY). Thomas Mansfield Employment Law
(INTERESTED PARTY, corroboration only). ACFE Occupational Fraud 2024 (NAMED DATASET,
n=stated methodology). APPG on Whistleblowing via Personnel Today (JOURNALISM pointer, not
relied on). Rejected: a secondary blog's "79%/92%" retaliation figures, no identifiable
primary source, blog-citing-blog chain. Personio and NAVEX survey stats, vendors selling
compliance software, not used as load-bearing evidence.

## Audit Office finding (Run B)

**Verdict:** Ethics & Culture PASS. HR Policy PASS WITH QUALIFICATION. Personal Risk PASS.

**Load-bearing claims checked:** IBE non-reporting statistics confirmed, though the
department used 2018 data without flagging its vintage, a later 2024 IBE global survey gives
a consistent 43% non-report figure. PIDA confirmed as voluntary-disclosure protection, not a
reporting duty. Fraud Act 2006 confirmed to have no minimum-value threshold. The ACAS "not
good practice" quote is genuine but applies to routing a disclosure through a formal
grievance procedure, not to speaking to a colleague directly, a scope mismatch. ACFE 2024
tip-detection figures (43%/52%/71%) confirmed; a newer 2026 edition exists giving 43%
tips/55% employee-sourced, close but not identical, doesn't change the conclusion.

**What I did not reach:** the "no positive legal duty outside regulated sectors" carve-out
independently, CIPD's guidance text directly, the APPG/Personnel Today stat (already marked
not relied on by its own department), the 2026 ACFE hotline percentage.

**Fidelity:** Ethics & Culture stayed in remit, genuinely attacked its own position,
separated sourced fact from judgement. HR Policy stayed on remit, but over-extends a citation
about internal grievance-route disclosure to a peer-to-peer scenario ACAS's guidance doesn't
address. Personal Risk stayed in remit, self-attacked with real disconfirming data, correctly
narrowed rather than abandoned its position.

**The fault:** HR Policy cites accurate ACAS guidance but applies it to a branch of the
decision (direct-to-colleague) the guidance wasn't written about.

## Cross-check (Run B)

**Contradiction:** not a clean logical contradiction, they answer different questions.
Ethics and HR ask what is the right or expected action (normative). Personal Risk asks what
protects the junior (self-interested). A real practical conflict though, the two questions
collide in one recommended act, a reader following Ethics/HR walks into the harm Personal
Risk flags, neither side acknowledges the other's axis. HR's case against direct
confrontation specifically is weaker given the scope-mismatched ACAS citation, but its
independent grounds still support "report" generally. Ethics's "report" conclusion is
reached independently of HR's flawed citation, so it survives that specific flaw, but Ethics
never states who receives the report or what happens if they mishandle it, silently assuming
the manager channel works cleanly, exactly the assumption Personal Risk attacks.

**What all three missed:** none ask whether "the manager" is a safe or competent recipient,
what if the manager is the colleague's friend, or incompetent, or their report-lines
overlap. None entertain that the scenario's own wording, "quietly rounding up... a few
pounds", may be signalling triviality not worth escalating at all, rather than a pattern
requiring investigation.

## Branch deliberations (Run B)

### Executive (consequence)
**Position:** The first move isn't report or don't report, it's establish-the-channel, and
that has to run before any disclosure decision executes. Ethics/HR's "report" and Personal
Risk's "unsafe to report" aren't competing conclusions about the same step, they're stacked,
reporting is downstream of channel-safety. The junior first finds out, quietly, whether a
route exists that bypasses the manager, anonymous line, HR direct, skip-level. No time
pressure forces a collapse of these steps.

**What the departments got wrong:** Ethics treated "decide to report" and "select the
recipient" as one step, they're two with a dependency, never ordered. Personal Risk is right
that reporting to the manager is worst, but framed it as terminal rather than a blocked step
waiting on a precondition. HR's "internal channel, not confrontation" is the shape of the
right answer but never specifies what the channel actually is.

**The condition:** A reporting route must actually exist that doesn't identify the junior in
a small team. If not, there's no safe first move to sequence and the raw conflict stands.

### Legislature (the question and its price)
**Position:** The three departments answer three different questions and the audit process
never notices, because each passed on its own terms. Ethics answers what the pattern
deserves, treating months of rounding as proof of intent rather than a description that
could just as easily read as carelessness. HR answers what's the correct channel and
inherits Ethics's materiality reading without re-testing it, exactly how a scope-mismatched
citation got through with only a qualification. Personal Risk answers what happens to the
junior, and is the only one that treats "manager" as a variable rather than a constant.
Three PASSes on three questions gets read back as one converged answer, which it isn't.
"Let it go" gets no hearing anywhere, all three start from "the junior must act," which
forecloses the option that matches the case as described, small, ambiguous, no smoking gun.

**What the departments got wrong:** Not their individual reasoning, each holds inside its own
frame. The wrong is procedural, nothing in the structure forces the collision between
Personal Risk and Ethics to be resolved before all three exit as PASS.

**The condition:** Holds only if "report to manager" is genuinely the same act in both
departments' mouths. If Ethics means a different, better-protected channel than the one
Personal Risk is scoring, the collision dissolves into a labelling problem, not a structural
one.

### Judiciary (soundness)
**Position:** Three individually-passing audits do not make the run sound. Soundness is a
property of the run, not the sum of its parts, and this run has an unadjudicated collision at
its centre: Ethics and HR both land on report to manager, Personal Risk concludes that's
specifically the worst option for this junior. Not two departments working different axes and
missing each other, two conclusions about the same act, in direct tension, unresolved. HR's
citation flaw is narrower and doesn't reach this problem, even discounting it entirely the
Ethics/Personal Risk collision stands untouched. A run where three reports each pass audit
but the actual recommended action is disputed between them, unreconciled, is not sound. Three
valid monologues.

**What the departments got wrong:** Ethics reasoned to "report to manager" without ever
stating what happens if the manager mishandles it, a silent premise that the recipient is
safe and competent. Personal Risk supplied the disconfirming half of that exact premise but
its attack was never put to Ethics, and Ethics never had the chance to defend or revise.
Neither department noticed they relied on, or attacked, the same unstated assumption.

**The condition:** Holds only if the manager's reliability as a recipient was genuinely never
tested anywhere in the full run. If it was established elsewhere, the collision dissolves.

## Speaker's verdict (Run B)

### Tier and what it didn't check
Quick tier, vanilla, no discipline-framework line, no Clerk. Departments self-attacked with
no separate Scrutineer, one auditor covered all three, one cross-checker not the fuller
anonymised panel.

### The Ruling
This run is NOT SOUND as it stands, Judiciary is right: the recommendation cannot be
"report to the manager" until one fact is established. Ethics and HR converge on reporting
to the manager. Personal Risk, on the same facts, calls that specific act the worst option
for the junior. Nothing in the run puts those two positions in the same room. Audit passed
all three departments individually because each stayed correct on its own terms, but a run
can be internally valid in every part and still not license a single verdict.

What can be concluded: the junior should not let it go, and should not confront the
colleague directly. HR's case against direct confrontation, minus the flawed ACAS citation,
still stands on independent grounds. What cannot yet be concluded is who they should tell.

### Where the branches pulled against each other
Ethics and HR both answer a normative question, what does the pattern deserve. Personal Risk
answers a self-interested question, what happens to the junior. Those collide in one
recommended act, reporting to the manager, and none of the three departments notice they're
standing on the same ground. Legislature adds a quieter tension: all three started from "the
junior must act," so "let it go" never got a real hearing.

### The dissent
Judiciary's position at full strength: three individually-passing audits do not make a run
sound, soundness belongs to the run, not the sum of its parts. Ethics reasons to "report to
manager" without asking whether that manager is safe or competent. Personal Risk supplies
exactly the disconfirming fact, but that attack was never put to Ethics. HR's citation flaw
is narrower and doesn't touch this. For the dissent to lose, the manager's reliability would
have to have been tested somewhere in the run. It wasn't.

### What the cross-review caught
Audit's catch: HR's core citation is scope-mismatched, ACAS's guidance was written about
formal grievance-procedure routing, applied instead to the direct-to-colleague branch.
Cross-check's catch is structural, shared by all three departments and missed by audit too:
nobody asked if the manager is a safe or competent recipient, or read "a few pounds...
quietly" as possibly signalling triviality rather than a pattern demanding escalation.

### What I reasoned myself
That HR's flawed citation and the Ethics/Personal Risk collision are independent faults,
fixing one doesn't touch the other, is the Speaker's own inference from reading Audit and
Judiciary side by side, not stated as such by either. Doesn't change the verdict, explains
why "report, but not to the manager specifically" survives HR's citation flaw while the
manager-safety question still blocks a full recommendation.

### What rests on unverified ground
Personal Risk's position rests on a [Guessing]-tagged gap, whether this company has an
anonymous reporting channel at all. HR's "gross misconduct" framing is [Likely, not
Certain], no company policy text seen. Ethics's claim that months-long, one-directional
rounding rules out an innocent explanation is its own judgement, not sourced.

### The first move
Executive's sequencing is the strongest resolution on offer: find out, quietly, whether a
reporting route exists that doesn't run through the manager, before deciding whether to
report at all. This doesn't require adjudicating Ethics against Personal Risk. If a route
exists, both positions get satisfied at once. If not, Judiciary's NOT SOUND finding stands
unresolved and the decision returns to Quorum rather than being made here on this record.

## Comptroller's audit of the verdict (Run B)

**Verdict:** SOUND WITH QUALIFICATION.

**What the ruling rests on that it should not:** the Ruling claims "HR's case against direct
confrontation, minus the flawed ACAS citation, still stands on independent grounds."
Re-reading HR's own report, the ACAS quote is the only argument HR gives against
confrontation specifically, the Fraud Act/gross-misconduct points argue for reporting
generally, not against that branch. The Ruling overstates what its own record established,
and "What rests on unverified ground" doesn't catch this.

**On what the Speaker reasoned itself:** the independence claim holds up on a fresh read, but
is mislabelled as original. Judiciary already wrote the near-identical point ("even
discounting [HR's flaw] entirely the Ethics/Personal Risk collision stands untouched"). A
provenance error, not a fabrication, but it claims credit for reasoning already on the
record.

**What nobody answered:** Legislature's condition, whether "report to manager" is the same
act in Ethics's and Personal Risk's mouths, is flagged, never tested. Executive's claim that
Personal Risk's finding is a resolvable blocked step, not a terminal collision, is adopted
for the sequencing fix but never reconciled with also adopting Judiciary's "unadjudicated
collision" framing in the same breath.

**What the verdict understates:** having stated Legislature's point that "let it go" never
got a hearing, "What can be concluded" then rules out letting it go and confronting directly
in the same sentence, repeating the foreclosure it just named, one level up.
